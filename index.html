What the EU AI Act actually requires on gender bias in high-risk AI systems, and where those rules stop

A 2026 amendment changed how companies may check their AI for bias. Two experts explain what the law now says, what it leaves out, and why compliance is not the same as fairness.

By Khatimat Abilova · [Publication date]

“Legal compliance does not necessarily mean that an AI system is completely free from discrimination,” said Valentina Maglietta, a researcher at the Gender Five Plus NGO in Brussels.

That warning runs through the debate over the European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act. A 2026 amending regulation changed how companies may check their systems for bias. Yet both experts interviewed for this feature said that compliance cannot, by itself, promise fair outcomes for women.

What the law covers

The Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, sets harmonised rules for AI, with a high level of protection for health, safety and fundamental rights. This feature concerns its rules for high-risk systems. The 2026 amendment, known as the Digital Omnibus on AI, says its simplification measures should not reduce that protection.

Gender has no separate rulebook, on this account. Jordy Benooit, who works in EU public affairs, said gender discrimination is covered mainly through broader provisions on discrimination and fundamental rights, not through a dedicated gender-equality framework. Maglietta agreed, saying the Act is “primarily a regulatory framework for artificial intelligence”, not a comprehensive gender-equality instrument.

Maglietta called gender bias in AI “not simply a technical problem” but a human-rights issue, one that becomes particularly serious when decisions are automated or difficult to challenge. Benooit pointed to recruitment, healthcare and access to essential services as the likeliest trouble spots. A hiring tool might favour candidates who resemble past successful employees, he said. If a company has traditionally hired more men for senior positions, the system could reproduce that pattern.

What providers must do

Benooit said Article 10, on data governance, requires providers of high-risk systems to examine datasets for biases and take measures to detect, prevent and mitigate them, and that Article 10(3) addresses how representative datasets are. The amended Act itself confirms the link: the new Article 4a refers to bias detection and correction under Article 10(2), points (f) and (g).

Companies, Benooit said, “cannot simply collect data and assume that it is neutral”; they must examine whether women are underrepresented or disadvantaged. Maglietta went further. A large dataset is not enough, she said; the question is who is represented and whose experiences are missing, including where gender overlaps with ethnicity, disability or social background.

The 2026 change

The amending regulation inserts Article 4a. Providers of high-risk systems may “exceptionally” process special categories of personal data, “to the extent strictly necessary”, to detect and correct bias. Six conditions must all be met: no effective alternative, such as synthetic or anonymised data; technical limits on re-use and strong security; strict access controls; no transfer to other parties; deletion once the bias is corrected or the retention period ends; and records explaining why the processing was strictly necessary.

Article 4a(2) extends the same legal basis to deployers of high-risk systems and to providers and deployers of other AI systems and models, with the same safeguards. It “does not create any obligation” to carry out bias detection. Article 10(5) is deleted, and Articles 10(1) and 10(6) now point to Article 4a(1). A recital calls bias detection and correction a substantial public interest.

Maglietta said the change “does not make the use of sensitive data automatic or unrestricted.”

Timing matters too. Under amended Article 113, the Act’s Chapter III requirements for Annex III high-risk systems apply from 2 December 2027, replacing the original date of 2 August 2026. Benooit said recruitment tools fall under Annex III. If he is right, the hiring-tool rules apply from the later date.

Where the rules stop

“A system might meet certain technical requirements while still producing unequal outcomes in practice.”

Valentina Maglietta, researcher at Gender Five Plus NGO, Brussels

Not every system is high-risk, both experts noted. Compliance is not the same as equal outcomes, either. “The legislation does not guarantee that an AI system will produce equal outcomes for men and women,” Benooit said.

Enforcement is a further gap. Regulators need expertise and resources to spot discrimination that is not immediately visible, Benooit said. Maglietta added that people harmed by an algorithm may lack the information or resources to prove it, and that the Act does not completely address the social and institutional inequalities that shape how AI is built.

Both said the fundamental-rights impact assessment in Article 27 applies only to specified deployers and uses, not universally. Benooit added that Article 26(7) requires deployers to inform workers’ representatives before using a high-risk system at work.

Beyond the Act

The Act sits alongside other law. Article 4a’s conditions apply in addition to the EU’s data-protection rules, according to the amended text. Benooit said companies must also meet existing duties under the GDPR and EU equality directives. Maglietta said compliance with the AI Act does not displace other equality and fundamental-rights obligations, and that the Charter of Fundamental Rights should guide interpretation.

What should change

“… innovation and fundamental-rights protection develop together, rather than treating them as competing objectives.”

Jordy Benooit, EU public affairs

Benooit named implementation as the biggest challenge, and called for greater awareness among companies of how historical inequalities become embedded in algorithms. Maglietta set out four steps, saying the aim should be AI that respects equality and human dignity, not merely AI that is technically efficient: gender-sensitive impact assessments and lifecycle evaluation; more diverse design teams; a voice for affected groups; and effective enforcement with accessible ways to challenge decisions.

Myth vs what the law says

Backed by the amended law

What the law says

Article 10(5) was deleted, but the amending regulation inserts Article 4a, which sets a limited legal basis and six conditions for processing sensitive data to detect and correct bias. Articles 10(1) and 10(6) now refer to Article 4a(1). The amending regulation also says its simplification should not reduce the level of protection for fundamental rights.

What experts add

Benooit said Article 10(5) was deleted because its subject matter is now addressed in Article 4a. Maglietta said the provision moved from Article 10(5) into Article 4a.

Backed by the amended law

What the law says

Providers of high-risk systems may process special categories of personal data only “exceptionally” and “to the extent strictly necessary”. All six conditions in Article 4a(1) must be met, in addition to the EU data-protection rules.

What experts add

Maglietta said Article 4a does not make the use of sensitive data automatic or unrestricted. Benooit said it applies only where strict conditions and safeguards are met.

Partly backed

What the law says

Article 4a(2) lets deployers of high-risk systems, and providers and deployers of other AI systems and models, process sensitive data for bias detection under the same safeguards. It “does not create any obligation” to do so. Whether other duties apply cannot be checked here, because the original Act is not among our sources.

What experts add

Both experts said not every AI system is high-risk. Benooit described the Article 10 data duties as falling on providers of high-risk systems.

Expert view

What the law says

The amending regulation, the only legal text used for this article, does not say that compliance rules out discrimination. The original Act was not available to check.

What experts add

Maglietta said an AI system could comply and still discriminate against women, and that compliance does not displace other equality and fundamental-rights laws. Benooit said compliance does not automatically mean discrimination has disappeared.

Expert view

What the law says

The amending regulation’s bias provisions speak of discrimination prohibited under Union law and do not single out gender. A recital describes bias detection as protecting people from adverse effects, including discrimination.

What experts add

Maglietta called the Act primarily a regulatory framework for AI, not a comprehensive gender-equality instrument. Benooit said it sets no specific gender-equality framework for every AI system and relies on existing equality law.

Partly backed

What the law says

Under amended Article 113, the high-risk requirements in Chapter III, Sections 1 to 3, apply to Annex III systems from 2 December 2027. Recital 40 gives the original date as 2 August 2026.

What experts add

Benooit said Annex III, point 4, covers recruitment and selection. Our sources cannot check that, so the hiring-tool timing holds only if he is right.

Timeline

Dates as stated in the amending regulation.

  1. 1 August 2024

    The AI Act enters into force

    The amending regulation records that Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force on this date.

  2. 2 February 2025

    Chapters I and II apply

    Amended Article 113 says these chapters apply from this date, with listed exceptions in Article 5 that apply from 2 December 2026.

  3. 8 July 2026

    The Digital Omnibus on AI is adopted

    Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 is dated 8 July 2026 and amends the AI Act.

  4. 24 July 2026

    Published in the Official Journal

    The regulation enters into force on the third day following publication.

  5. 2 August 2026

    The original general date of application

    Recital 40 gives this as the initial date for the high-risk obligations in Chapter III, Sections 1 to 3. The amendment replaces it.

  6. 2 December 2027

    Annex III high-risk obligations apply

    Chapter III, Sections 1 to 3 apply from this date to systems classified as high-risk under Article 6(2) and Annex III.

  7. 2 August 2028

    Annex I high-risk obligations apply

    The same sections apply from this date to systems classified as high-risk under Article 6(1) and Annex I.
